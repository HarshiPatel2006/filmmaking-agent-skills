# Axis and reorientation examples

Original schematic illustrating the underlying geometry discussed by Katz (KT 142–157) and Mascelli (MC 90, 110, 130). Not copied from either book.

```
World overhead plan, before a conversation shifts:

            opposite side (unestablished)

 A looking right  ----------------> B looking left
       A ========================== B
                 active axis

         C1        Cwide        C2
                  established camera side
```

- A camera near B looking toward A and a camera near A looking toward B may provide complementary singles. Predict actual screen directions from the viewpoint rather than assuming the overhead diagram itself is the image.
- If the characters stay fixed and the camera moves to the unestablished side between cuts, their apparent facing may reverse. A continuous camera move that shows the crossing, a clear shared shot establishing new positions, or a newly established gaze relationship can reorient viewers.
- With a third participant C, the active axis may become B–C. Mark the new target and show the shift of attention before cutting tightly. Do not keep a single axis for every possible exchange if staging actually changes.
- In intentional discontinuity or montage, disorientation may serve the scene. Record why and test whether viewers experience the intended effect rather than an accidental geographic contradiction.

Return to the location map when axes conflict. For moving subjects, separately track movement direction, entries/exits and eyelines.
