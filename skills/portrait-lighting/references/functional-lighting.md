# Functional lighting roles

Read this when a portrait or close-up needs more precise control than a simple key/fill description. Alton describes eight functional roles in the close-up chapter (AL, local PDF 139–146); the useful lesson is decomposition by purpose, not a requirement to use eight lamps.

| Role | Decision to make | Risk |
|---|---|---|
| Fill | Decide how much shadow detail remains; use bounce, ambient light or negative fill as appropriate. | Flattening all modeling. |
| Key | Establish dominant direction, quality and facial form. | Conflicting apparent sources. |
| Local filler | Correct an unwanted specific shadow without raising the entire fill level. | Unmotivated second shadow or unnatural uplight. |
| Clothing accent | Keep relevant texture/shape visible without stealing attention from face. | Bright costume overpowering expression. |
| Back light | Separate subject silhouette if needed. | Artificial halo or washed-out edge. |
| Kicker | Add a localized side/rear accent where it improves form. | Excessive specular highlight. |
| Eye reflection | Preserve a believable reflected source in the eye when its absence weakens expression. | A conspicuous reflection disconnected from the environment. |
| Background | Organize tone and attention behind the face. | Competing highlights or discontinuity from wider view. |

Start with key and ambient environment, review the actual face, then add or subtract only what solves a visible problem. Specify source positions in a consistent world or subject-relative coordinate system (e.g. azimuth, elevation and distance relative to the lens and face), not vague left/right terms. Alton's clock-position illustrations (PDF 133–138) are a notation aid; the exact positions depend on the face, camera and motivation. Compare the master and close-up at intended display conditions. An actual physical rig must meet current electrical, rigging and eye-safety practices.
