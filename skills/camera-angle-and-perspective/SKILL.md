# Camera angle and perspective

## Inputs and deliverable

- Accept: subject; scene goal; geometry; adjacent shots.
- Produce: camera specification with viewpoint, height, orientation and rationale.
- If critical inputs are missing, state assumptions, distinguish planning from observed footage, and mark any untestable decision for review. Do not invent measurements, assets or runtime results.

## Decision procedure

1. Identify whose relationship to the action should be expressed; compare neutral eye-level, higher, lower and oblique viewpoints.
2. Consider the surfaces, overlap, background, perspective and relative height visible from each position.
3. Use high or low angles for actual visual consequences, not automatic dominance or weakness; compare emotional and informational effects.
4. Change camera position to establish the perspective first, then select field of view and framing.
5. Check eyelines, horizon, screen direction and neighboring shot contrast; justify any intentionally disorienting viewpoint.

## Diagnose and correct

A high angle may communicate geography, not weakness. Avoid symbolic angle shortcuts and physically impossible perspective descriptions.

## Output checks

- Deliver the specified artifact, with decisions tied to identifiable scenes, beats or shots when relevant.
- Show evidence or reasoning for the selected approach and at least one plausible alternative when the decision is consequential; state whether it is planned or actually observed.
- Verify the specific failure mode above is not present, or document the creative reason and the test that would demonstrate success.
- Use the original practice case below to check that the procedure produces a concrete, reviewable result rather than a definition or generic advice.

## Practice case

Film a child and adult at a table; choose a viewpoint that communicates their relation without distorting either face unnecessarily.
