# Timing a sequence

Katz's temporal connections and shot-flow discussion (KT 158–185) and Alton's visual music analogy (AL 198–203) encourage evaluating relationships rather than isolated images. The following audio-led procedure is a modern application; it is not a measured timing rule from the books.

1. Mark actual vocal, instrumental, performance and dramatic events. Maintain separate tracks for beat candidates and confirmed events.
2. For each planned shot note minimum readable duration, important action or reaction, likely cut opportunity, and available footage handles.
3. Try a phrase-led version and a more accent-led version. Compare narrative comprehension, feeling, and the use of rests. Do not cut on every beat by default.
4. If an audio master defines the timeline, use measured frame-compatible edit boundaries and preserve the continuous master. Do not fabricate a lyric alignment or force shots into fixed 5-second chunks.
5. Use the same scene at different timings to isolate the effect of a cut. A shot held through a reaction can be more meaningful than cutting immediately when the speaking or singing phrase changes.

Original example (fictional timestamps, not a real audio transcription): at 0.0–2.8 s an empty hallway establishes absence; 2.8–6.9 s a character notices an object; 6.9–8.6 s detail reveals why it matters; 8.6–14.2 s reaction is held. The durations are deliberately unequal. Replace these with verified timing for the actual project. Check that the sequence communicates without the planning notes.
